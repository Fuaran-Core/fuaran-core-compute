# Compute benchmarks — 2026-11-23, Intel Core i7-8650U, Phase 725 (the pinned sine, cosine and arc tangents)

Phase 725 pins `sin`, `cos`, `sinCos`, `atan` and `atan2` in `Fuaran.Compute.Elementary` (`DECISIONS.md` D84).
This file records the measurement the decision rests on: each function's error against a fifty-digit reference by
range, its cost per value on .NET and under node beside the host's own function, and the candidates each choice was
made between.

## How it was taken

- Machine: Intel Core i7-8650U (4 cores, AVX2), Windows 11; .NET SDK 10.0.203 (Release, x64 native process),
  node v22.20.0 (Fable 5.0.0, the repository's tool manifest). The machine was running other work at the time, so
  the timings carry more noise than a quiet machine's would; the error figures and the byte comparison do not depend
  on the clock.
- Harness: the node leg's timing program (`benchmarks/Fuaran.Core.Compute.Benchmarks/Node`), family `elementary`
  (`../ElementaryBench.fs`), the same program on both hosts:
  - .NET: `dotnet run -c Release --project benchmarks/Fuaran.Core.Compute.Benchmarks/Node -- 5 elementary > dotnet.txt`
  - node: `pwsh benchmarks/Fuaran.Core.Compute.Benchmarks/Node/run-node.ps1 -FableFrom . -Runs 5 -Only elementary > node.txt`
  - the check: the lines between `BEGIN trig-samples` and `END trig-samples`, through
    `python tests/Fuaran.Core.Compute.Tests/SpecialReference/trig_reference.py check`
- The sample is a seeded draw at 725 through the pinned Park–Miller generator, so it is the same 55,998 inputs on
  every host:
  - `sin` and `cos`, 19,000 each: 3,000 uniform within `π/4`, where no reduction runs; 3,000 uniform over
    `[−100, 100]` and 3,000 over `[−2²⁰, 2²⁰]`, where the three pieces of `π/2` reduce; 3,000 log-uniform from `2²⁰`
    to `2⁵³` and 3,000 from `2⁵³` to the largest double, in both signs, where the digits of `2/π` reduce; and the
    doubles nearest the first 2,000 multiples of `π/2` with the double above each, where the remainder cancels.
  - `atan`, 7,000: 4,000 uniform over `[−4, 4]`, which every arm meets, and 3,000 log-uniform from `2⁻⁴⁰` to `2⁷⁰` in
    both signs.
  - `atan2`, 10,998: 8,000 points uniform over `[−3, 3]²`, scored apart by the sign of `x`, and 2,998 whose
    coordinates lie anywhere in the exponent range and within `2⁴⁰` of each other.
- Each case: a warm-up of at least half a second, then the median of 5 samples, each enough passes to span fifty
  milliseconds (Phase 593's timer, unchanged).
- The reference is the script's own: exact rational and fixed-point integer arithmetic at 620 fractional bits and
  more, in Python's standard library, rounded once to the nearest double. Its `π/2` in pieces, its digits of `2/π`
  and its split arc tangents are the constants fdlibm publishes, bit for bit. The distance is in units in the last
  place of the correctly rounded double.
- The two captures' sample lines, the package's answers and every candidate's, are compared byte for byte: that is
  the byte-identity claim. They were identical, all 111,996.

What each row decided:

- **The kernel.** fdlibm's minimax kernels and the Taylor kernels `Special` already held are as accurate as each other
  within `π/4`: the same worst sine, and four hundredths of a unit between the worst cosines. The Taylor kernels ship,
  because they are the ones the special functions' released answers were computed with.
- **The reduction.** `π/2` in two pieces with the remainder rounded to one double is the cheaper form. Inside `2²⁰` it
  is a third of a unit worse on an ordinary argument and has nothing to answer with beside a multiple of `π/2`, where
  the remainder is the difference of two nearly equal numbers; beyond `2²⁰` its multiple is no longer exact and the
  answer is noise. It saves nothing either: 26.9 ns against 26.6.
- **The arc tangent's arms.** Three arms, with the constant added after the polynomial is rounded, round twice: 1.43
  units against 0.74.
- **The quotient.** The arc tangent of the rounded quotient, taken from `π` after it is rounded, is 1.2 units in both
  half-planes. Carrying the quotient's remainder and reading `π` less each arm's constant brings both under 0.78.

Reading the cost:

- **About twice the host's on an ordinary argument.** The pinned sine and cosine are 26 ns against the host's 13 up to
  `2²⁰`, and 10 to 12 against 4 within `π/4`; under node, 69 against 34. `atan` is the host's cost; `atan2` is a half
  more. That is the price of an answer the next host reproduces.
- **`sinCos` is cheaper than two calls** once the argument is reduced: 40 ns against 54 for `sin` and `cos` apart up
  to `2²⁰`. The row at 100 on .NET, and the two rows well above their neighbours under node (`sinCos` beside the
  multiples, `atan2` with `x` positive), are the machine's other work and not the functions'.
- **Ten times more beyond `2²⁰`.** The exact reduction by the digits of `2/π` costs about 260 ns on .NET and 850 ns
  under node, against 26 and 69 for the three pieces below `2²⁰`. That is why the pieces are kept for the arguments a
  caller usually passes, and why the digits are not the one reduction for every argument: they would make every
  ordinary sine ten times dearer to save one branch. Beyond `2²⁰` the cost buys a correct answer where the cheaper
  form has none.
- **The minimax kernels are quicker and no more accurate.** The kernel candidates above are the bare kernels, with no
  table and no reduction in front of them, so the gap is wider than the kernels' own; the Taylor kernels sum nine
  terms where the minimax ones sum six. D84 records why that is not taken.
