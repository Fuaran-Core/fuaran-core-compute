# Compute benchmarks — 2026-09-27, Snapdragon X Elite (X1E80100), Phase 267

A follow-up to `2026-09-27-snapdragon-x1e80100-phase-266.md`. The "before" column is the tree at
`f40be00` — Phases 262 to 266 landed, so the row form with array rows, the hash join, the one-pass
pivot, the allocation-free grouping comparer and the compiled expression tree — and the "after"
column is that tree plus Phase 267: the evaluator's frame column-major and typed (one vector per
column beside a validity mask, a selection vector for the rows a step keeps and their order, a
`Derive` that adds one vector and shares the rest, the origin `Cell list` kept beside each vector
the boundary unpacked so the boundary out can hand it back), the compiled tree reading typed
vectors unboxed, and a source prepared once (`DataFrame.prepare` / `evalPrepared`). Same machine,
same instrument, same corpus; the WHOLE corpus is re-measured on both trees and on both hosts,
because a representation change reaches every verb.

| node instrument | the `Node/` harness: two warm-up calls, calls per sample doubled until a sample spans 50 ms, then ten measured runs |

**The figure is the median**, as in the earlier files: BenchmarkDotNet's `Median` column on .NET,
the harness's median on node. `Allocated` is managed bytes per operation on .NET; node carries the
median only.

**How the runs were taken.** A first before/after pair was measured while this phase's own test
suite and node compile ran on the same machine, and it disagreed with itself by up to 80% on the
grouping shapes; it is discarded. The figures below are pairs taken with nothing else of the
operator's or this session's running: the before tree's whole corpus and node leg (the tree
exported to a short path beside an empty local feed folder), then the after tree's, its corpus
check (`--check`) green before anything was timed. The `Error` column of both .NET runs was under
5% of the mean on every row but the two 100,000-row window and sort shapes. A ratio is the after
median over the before median.

The hand arm (arrays and loops, full recompute) is unchanged by this phase and moves within noise.
The two 100,000-row cases are the phase's clearest figures. At `f40be00` each spent most of its
time in gen-2 collections of the row arrays and the transposes at the `Table` boundary. Now the
frame unpacks each column once into a typed vector; a `Derive` writes one `int[]` beside a mask;
the boundary out hands the consumer's own `Cell list` back for every column the pipeline did not
touch (the origin kept beside the vector), so `lines` boxes only its two derived columns; and the
`GroupBy` boxes only its key cells and the one aggregated column. What remains of the allocation
is the derived and grouped output — one `Cell list` per produced column, which the consumer
contract still requires — and the row-form `Cell list` of the input, which the benchmark builds
per operation.

| filter-groupby-filter: full | 5.99 | 3.82 | 0.64 | 8,218.81 KB | 7,034.6 KB |
| filter-groupby-filter: refresh | 10.00 | 10.50 | 1.05 | 14,526.02 KB | 14,525.92 KB |

The refresh rows go through the incremental seam, whose walk this phase does not touch (it still
reads array rows through `RowAccess.rows` and evaluates through `evalResolved`); they are the
control, and they move within noise with their allocations byte-identical. `filter-sort-limit:
full` is the frame's shape: the `Filter` produces a selection, the `Sort` a permutation of it under
typed key comparators, the `Limit` a slice, and no row is copied until the ten survivors are packed
back — 13.7 MB becomes 1.7 MB, most of which is the input table the benchmark rebuilds. The suite's
own Debug-build figure for the one-comparison top-N case moved the same way (full 91 ms to 22 ms at
20,000 rows, the refresh 57 ms to 43 ms), which is why that case's "the refresh wins" assertion is
now the bounded loss `ScalingTests.cheapRefreshLossBound` states, as Phase 265's grouping cases
already were.

The gathering verbs are at parity on the clock (the join's 12% is within the two runs' spread on
that row) and allocate 10 to 20% more: a `Join`, a `Pivot`, a `Distinct` and a `Window` read their
rows through the selection, and a cell read from a typed vector is boxed per read, where the row
form held every cell already boxed. `GroupBy` is the exception, deliberately: it gathers its
members as physical row indices and boxes only the key cells and the one column each aggregate
reads, which is why `filter-groupby` above improves. A first cut of this phase gathered full rows
there too and measured `filter-groupby: full` at 1.8× the before figure under contention; the index
gather is what brought it under. Taking the same step for the other gathering verbs — or, better,
keying them on typed values so nothing is boxed — is the representation work the next phases own,
and this file is the number they start from. The window shape is dominated by the partition's
`List.sortWith` and its per-row list building, which this phase left in the row form on purpose: it
is what the public `windowStep` twins share.

## Memory per integer cell at rest

Measured ad hoc (the harness has no shape for "memory at rest"): one `IntType` column of 100,000
rows, `GC.GetTotalMemory(true)` before and after, the table alive throughout so the delta is the
prepared form alone. `Prepared` holds the table by reference and never copies it, and the frame's
origin reference to the column's list is the same object again.

| | Bytes | Per cell |
|---|---:|---:|
| the `Table` itself (a `Cell list` of present ints) | 5,599,984 | 56.00 B |
| `DataFrame.prepare` of it — the frame beside that table | 500,256 | 5.0026 B |
| a table with every fourth cell null | 5,000,192 | 50.00 B |
| its prepared form | 504,232 | 5.0423 B |

Five bytes a cell — four for the `int32`, one for the `bool` mask entry — plus about 260 bytes of
fixed overhead (two array headers, the `Prepared` and `Frame` records, the schema), against the
56 bytes the plan's audit named for a present integer cell in a list. The target was at or under
five bytes a cell; a bitmask would take the mask's byte to an eighth of one, and is the later
refinement the plan names.

## node

The same corpus through the package's own sources as Fable compiles them, under node v24.16.0, on
the quiet machine, both trees. Numeric vectors compile to typed arrays and the mask to an array of
booleans; nothing here uses spans, intrinsics, pooled buffers or threads. The corpus agreements
(the hand arm against the evaluator, each refresh against its full evaluation, each shape's output
size) held on both trees before anything was timed.

**Two readings of the after tree are shown, because the first one changed the design.** The
phase's first after tree kept no origin lists and sorted an `int[]` in place. Under V8 that read
1.5 to 2× slower than the before tree on every wide-output shape and 2.8× slower on
`filter-sort-limit`, while the grouped shapes improved — a pattern the .NET figures did not show.
Two mechanisms explained it and both were removed: every boxed output cell survives into the
output list and is promoted by the scavenger, so the boundary out was re-boxing every unchanged
column (the origin list beside each vector now goes back untouched while the selection is the
identity), and a typed array's sort with a comparator is the engine's slow path (the sort now
orders a plain list of positions under the same total order). The "after" column is the final
tree; the "first after" column is kept so the next reader can see what each fix bought.

Under node the small sheets and the grouping pipelines sit at parity, the 100,000-row sheets are
0.6 of the before tree, and the shapes that gather full rows — the join, the high-cardinality
group-by (every row opens a slot, so the key probe is the whole cost), the pivot and the window —
are 1.3 to 1.7× slower: a cell boxed per read is a fresh object per cell under V8 where the row
form read an existing one, and the JavaScript engine's allocator feels that where the .NET one does
not. That is the same gather the .NET section names as the next phases' work; on node it is the
first thing to take, and the `Window`'s partition gather (100,000 rows through `rowsOf`, then
`List.sortWith` over them) the first shape to take it on.
